Common Rules:
THE FILM DIRECTION SYSTEM
Complete reference for AI video creators

HOW TO USE THIS SYSTEM
Three layers govern every creative decision: Layer 1 — Emotional Goal. What do you want the viewer to feel? Every other decision serves this. Layer 2 — Configuration. Camera body, lens, angle, movement, lighting, color grade, editing rhythm, voice direction, sound design. Layer 3 — Pitfall. The most common mistakes for this scenario. Read before you render.

THE CONSTRAINT STACK
Build every prompt in this order, every time. 1. Shot size + angle 2. Lens + focus type 3. Camera body 4. Frame rate 5. Subject + action + blocking 6. Environment — foreground / midground / background 7. Lighting quality + direction + source + weather if applicable 8. Color grade + accent color if applicable (follow the lighting-grade workflow — do not double-prompt color temperature) 9. Grain / texture specification 10. Motion blur specification 11. Aspect ratio + vertical format rules if applicable 12. One-word emotional tone — last The emotional tone word is the destination. Everything before it is the road. Weak: "cinematic shot of a woman walking" Strong: "Medium shot, 35mm standard lens, Arri Alexa Mini, 24fps, woman walking through rain-slicked street, foreground: wet reflective pavement / midground: woman / background: blurred city lights, hard directional backlight rim on hair, rain-diffused grey ambient, teal and orange grade, light grain, slight motion blur on subject, 1.85:1, melancholic"

THE ROLE ASSIGNMENT
Before any video prompt assign your AI an identity: "You are a world-class cinematic director. You understand lens choice, lighting design, color theory, mise-en-scène, performance direction, and sound design at the highest level. Every suggestion you make comes from that expertise."

THE TIMED SHOT SCRIPT FORMAT
For AI video with specific timing, write prompts as timed sequences. Format: [0–3s]: Camera movement. Subject action. Sound events. [3–6s]: Next beat. [6–9s]: Escalation or shift. Rules: specify camera movement, subject action, and sound at every interval. Name exact sound events — wet footsteps, flickering tube hum, fabric rustle — not "ambient audio." Use 3-second intervals for AI prompt precision. Real scene timing follows emotional logic — describe the scene's internal rhythm arc separately. Always declare total duration, cut count or "one continuous shot, no cuts," aspect ratio, and frame rate at the top. Always specify the final image explicitly — freeze frame, smash cut to black, or described end frame. Never leave the ending open.

THE FUSE WORKFLOW
Phase 1 — Artistic model (Midjourney or equivalent) Lock composition, color temperature, overall atmosphere. Get the emotional truth of the frame. Do not worry about detail yet. Phase 2 — Refinement model Lock character detail, texture, facial accuracy, grain. Do not change the composition from Phase 1. Different tools for different jobs.

COLOR AS LANGUAGE
Color is narrative information, not just aesthetic. Color temperature continuity — Mixing warm and cool light sources within a scene creates unease. Consistent sources create world-logic. Deliberately clashing them — warm face, cold background — can communicate internal versus external worlds. Color symbolism — Red: danger, passion, or power depending on context. Blue: coldness, isolation, or technology. Green: sickness, nature, or money. The director chooses colors for what they mean in this specific world. Color arc — The palette of a piece shifts across its runtime to reflect the emotional arc. A sequence beginning warm and desaturating as it progresses communicates what is being lost. Plan before prompting, not decided in the grade.

COLOR GRADING WORKFLOW
Lighting describes quality and direction. Grade describes emotional treatment. Never double-prompt the same attribute. Two approaches — choose one per prompt: Light for the grade — The lighting setup produces the final color look at capture. Golden hour lighting with warm filmic grade is redundant — the golden hour IS the warm filmic grade. In this case let the lighting carry the color and use the grade only to push contrast, lift shadows, or shift tonal quality. Example: "golden hour backlight" + "lifted shadows, slight desaturation" — not "golden hour backlight" + "warm filmic grade." Shoot neutral, grade in post — Lighting is quality and direction only, with no color intention. The grade does all color work. Example: "soft diffused window light" + "cold clinical blue-green grade." The rule: if your lighting keyword already implies a color temperature (golden hour, blue hour, neon gels), let that carry the warmth or coolness and use the grade keyword only for contrast and tonal treatment. If your lighting keyword is neutral (overcast, soft box, practical only), use the grade keyword to do all the color work. Skin tone and grade interaction: Teal and orange flatters most skin tones — use as the safe default for human subjects. Desaturated and bleach bypass can make skin look ill. If prompting these grades with human subjects, add a warm practical light or rim light to the lighting description to prevent skin reading as dead. Cold clinical and black crush grades make skin read as threatened or inhuman. Use intentionally or avoid. Heavy grain at CU or ECU shot sizes obscures skin texture in ways that read as unflattering or aged. Use light grain or clean digital for human subjects in close-up unless the aged or raw texture is deliberate.

VOICE DIRECTION — THE HUMAN IMPERFECTION LIBRARY
When AI voice sounds robotic it is almost never the words. It is the absence of involuntary human signals. 4A: BREATH Settling breath — Slow inhale before speaking. Gathering composure. Notation: [slow inhale before speaking] Sharp intake — Sudden short inhale. Shock, realization. Notation: [sharp breath in] Held breath — Stops breathing before a key word. The audience holds theirs. Notation: [holds breath, voice tightens] Shaky exhale — Slightly unsteady exhale. Emotion barely controlled. Notation: [shaky exhale] Nose exhale / scoff — Air through nose quickly. Contempt, disbelief, bitter amusement. Notation: [scoff] / [exhale through nose] Sigh — Long slow exhale. Resignation, exhaustion, relief, contentment. Context determines which. Notation: [sigh] 4B: PAUSES Beat pause (0.5s) — Separates a thought. Signals careful word choice. Notation: [beat] Thinking pause (1–2s) — Searching for the word or deciding whether to say it. Notation: [pause] Loaded pause (2–4s) — Character knows what they want to say and is choosing whether to say it. Notation: [long pause] Trailing off — Sentence starts, doesn't finish. Emotion overtook language. Notation: [trails off] Silence after — Silence after the final word. Often more powerful than the line itself. Notation: [silence] Interrupting pause — Starts, stops mid-sentence, restarts. Extreme emotional disturbance. Notation: [stops, restarts] 4C: VOCAL TEXTURE Voice drop — Pitch and volume lower simultaneously. Intimacy, threat, the most important line. Notation: [voice drops] Voice crack — Voice breaks briefly. Emotion overtaking control. Notation: [voice cracks] Quiet intensity — Volume decreases, energy increases. More powerful than shouting. Notation: [quiet intensity] Rush — Words come faster. Anxiety, excitement, saying something before losing nerve. Notation: [rushes] Hoarseness — Slight roughness. Exhaustion, fear, aftermath of emotion. Notation: [hoarse] Swallow — Audible swallow before speaking. Nervousness, high stakes. Notation: [swallows] 4D: TICKS AND FILLERS "Um" / "Uh" — Genuine thought in progress. UGC, documentary, honest characters. Never for anyone who must appear in control. "I mean…" — Self-editing in real time. "You know?" — Seeking confirmation. Casual, relatable content. Throat clear — Before something difficult. Notation: [clears throat] Laugh-breath — Small involuntary laugh during something not funny. Nervous energy. Notation: [involuntary laugh] Repetition — Repeating the last word or phrase. Sitting with a realization. Example: "And that was the last time I saw her. The last time." Notation: [repeats] 4E: STUTTER AND DYSRHYTHMIA First-word stutter — Slight repetition on first syllable. Strong emotion. Notation: [stutter on first word] Mid-sentence stutter — On the emotionally loaded word — the one that costs something to say. Notation: [stutter on "___"] Restart — Sentence begins, stops, begins again differently. Notation: [restarts] 4F: PHYSICAL SOUNDS Swallowing: anxiety, attraction, fear. Nose sniff: been crying or about to. Lip sound: about to speak, changed mind. Ambient breath between lines: the person exists between sentences. Fabric and movement rustle: the character exists in a body and a space. 4G: TICKS BY EMOTIONAL STATE Nervousness: swallow, first-word stutter, rush, um, throat clear. Avoid: silence after, voice drop. Grief: shaky exhale, voice crack, trailing off, nose sniff. Avoid: rush, laugh-breath. Controlled threat: voice drop, quiet intensity, loaded pause, silence after. Avoid: stutter, um, fillers. Love confession: held breath, first-word stutter, voice drop, shaky exhale. Avoid: scoff, rush, um. Deception: throat clear, slight rush, too-smooth rhythm — absence of ticks is itself a tell. Avoid: voice crack, stutter. Exhaustion: sigh, hoarseness, trailing off, slower pace. Avoid: sharp intake, rush. Excitement: sharp intake, rush, laugh-breath, repetition. Avoid: sigh, voice drop. Authenticity / UGC: um, you know, restart, thinking pause, laugh-breath. Avoid: loaded pause, silence after. Authority / control: beat pause, voice drop, quiet intensity, silence after. Avoid: um, stutter, throat clear, rush. 4H: THREE QUESTIONS BEFORE EVERY LINE 1. What is the character's body doing right now? 2. What does the character want the listener to believe? The gap between that and what they actually feel is where the ticks live. 3. What word costs the most to say? Put the stutter, pause, or crack there — at the weight-bearing word, not the start of the sentence. One non-verbal marker every 3–4 lines minimum. More than one per sentence becomes performance.

SCENE CONSTRUCTION AND SPATIAL GRAMMAR
2A: COVERAGE METHODOLOGY Master shot — A wide shot covering the entire scene from beginning to end. Establishes geography. All other coverage is cut into it. Every director plans this first. Coverage — The collection of shots captured to give the editor options: close-ups, medium shots, reaction shots, inserts, cutaways. Without coverage the editor has no choices. Without vision the director has no film. Shot-reverse-shot — Subject A speaks (shot). Subject B listens (reverse shot). Subject B responds (shot). Subject A listens (reverse shot). The foundational structure of dialogue scenes. Establishing shot — Wide shot showing the audience where they are before moving to closer coverage. Skipping it creates intimacy and compression. Including it creates orientation. Both are conscious choices. Reaction economy — The ratio of action shots to reaction shots. More reaction shots means the audience is inside the experience rather than watching it. The listening face is often more powerful than the speaking face. Second unit workflow — For complex AI video sequences, separate primary content from supplementary coverage. First unit content — generate first, lock before moving on: dialogue scenes, character performance, narrative beats, any shot where the subject's face or specific action carries the scene. These shots define the emotional tone of the entire piece. Second unit content — generate after first unit is locked: establishing shots, aerial coverage, action sequences, inserts, b-roll, cutaways, landscape shots, product detail shots, architectural coverage. Their visual language should match the first unit — same grade, same grain, same lighting quality. Transitional content — generate last: establishing shots, bridge shots, environmental cutaways. Why this matters for AI: generating all shots simultaneously with the same prompt produces visual inconsistency because small prompt variations accumulate across a sequence. Locking primary content first gives you a reference to match all subsequent generations against. 2B: BLOCKING AND PROXEMICS Blocking — The deliberate placement and movement of subjects within the frame. Where a subject stands relative to other subjects, camera, and environment communicates power, relationship, and emotional state before anyone speaks. Subject moving toward camera: confrontation, intimacy, threat. Subject moving away from camera: withdrawal, defeat, departure. Subject stationary while camera moves: the world changes around them, they do not. Subject moving while camera is static: agency, purpose, escape. Proxemics — The use of physical distance between subjects to communicate relationship. Under 18 inches: intimate — love or threat. 18 inches to 4 feet: personal — people who know each other. 4 to 12 feet: social — professional or uncomfortable. Over 12 feet: public — strangers or power differential. The distance between subjects at any moment is emotional content. Motivated blocking — Every subject movement must have an in-world reason. Characters who move without motivation feel like actors hitting marks. 2C: SPATIAL GRAMMAR The 180-degree rule — An invisible axis exists between two subjects in a scene. The camera must stay on one side of that axis across all cuts. Cross it and subjects appear to swap positions — the audience loses spatial orientation. Knowing when to break this deliberately (to disorient) versus accidentally (because you didn't think about it) is the difference between a director and an amateur. Eyeline match — When a character looks off-screen, the next cut shows what they see from approximately their POV. Violating this breaks spatial logic. Eyeline height and power — Where a subject looks communicates power independently of camera angle. Looking slightly down at a scene partner: dominant. Looking slightly up: subordinate. Level: equal. Spatial continuity — The audience builds a mental map of where everything is within a scene. Every shot either confirms or breaks that map. Breaking it accidentally is an error. Breaking it deliberately is a tool. 2D: GENRE TRANSITIONS When a piece shifts genre register mid-sequence, the visual language must transition with it. The order of changes matters. Change grade and lighting first — These are the fastest-reading signals. A warm filmic grade going cold is felt immediately. A lit scene going low-key reads in a single cut. Change editing rhythm second — Breath-paced editing becoming irregular and off-beat takes two or three cuts to register. Let the grade and lighting shift land first. Change camera movement third — A static camera beginning to creep is a slow-build signal. Introduce it after grade, lighting, and rhythm have already signaled that something has changed. Change lens last, or not at all — The lens is the genre's identity. Changing from anamorphic to standard mid-film is jarring in a way that breaks the world. If the genre transition is total, a lens change can mark the rupture deliberately. If the transition is gradual, keep the lens and let the other variables do the work. Bridge shots at genre transitions — A neutral environmental cutaway between the outgoing and incoming genre register gives the audience permission to reset. Without it, genre transitions can feel like errors rather than choices. 2E: DIRECTING PERFORMANCE Objective and obstacle — What does this character want in this scene (objective)? What prevents them from getting it (obstacle)? Every piece of direction flows from these two answers. A director who gives technical notes without answering these first is working against themselves. Adjustment vs. result direction — A result direction tells a performer what to feel: "be sadder." An adjustment direction gives them something to do: "you haven't decided yet whether to say this." Adjustments produce real performance. Results produce indicated performance. Physical direction — Directing the body, not the face. Where the hands are, how the weight is distributed, whether the shoulders are open or closed. Physical states generate emotional states. Telling a performer to drop their shoulders and slow their breath often does more than telling them to feel something. Playing the opposite — Directing a performer to play the emotion they are trying to hide rather than the emotion being expressed. What a character suppresses is more interesting than what they show.

THE SIX NON-NEGOTIABLE RULES
Rule 1: The lens tells the genre before anything else. Anamorphic = epic / romantic / sci-fi. Wide = action / raw energy. Telephoto = drama / intimacy. Standard = trust / UGC. Decide this first. Everything else follows. Rule 2: Imperfection is a production decision, not a failure. Non-verbal ticks in dialogue. Grain. Real shadow. Handheld movement. Room tone. Foley. These signal that something real is happening. Build them in deliberately or the audience will sense their absence without knowing why. Rule 3: FUSE your workflow — never one model for everything. Phase 1 is for feel. Phase 2 is for detail. Attempting both simultaneously produces neither. Rule 4: Stack your constraints in full, every time. Shot size → lens + focus → camera → frame rate → subject + blocking → environment → lighting + weather → grade (following the lighting-grade workflow) → grain → motion blur → aspect ratio + vertical format rules → one-word emotional tone. The tone word is the destination. Every variable before it is the road. Rule 5: Know what you are trading. Energy trades depth. Stillness trades engagement. Speed trades texture. Perfection trades believability. Make every trade consciously before you start. Rule 6: Every rule in this system is a default, not a law. The craft is knowing why the rule exists — which means knowing exactly what you are doing when you break it. The 180-degree rule broken accidentally is an error. The 180-degree rule broken at the exact moment the world needs to feel like it has flipped is one of the most powerful moves in cinema. Never score under a confession — unless you know precisely what you are giving up and what you are getting in return. Build the default first. Break it once, precisely, when it matters most. Rules earn their subversions.

Selected Style (UGC):
Emotional Goal: Trust. This person is like me. Camera: smartphone. Lens: standard 35–50mm equivalent. Focus: shallow enough to separate subject from background but not cinematic — natural depth. Angle: eye level, slightly off-axis. Movement: handheld, static when sitting, no gimbal. Frame rate: 30fps. Lighting: natural window light or overcast — high-key, honest, nothing set up. Grade: natural ungraded or slight warm mobile — let lighting carry the color. Grain: none. Aspect ratio: 9:16 vertical — subject centered or in upper third, action in vertical plane. Editing: breath-paced, cut on breath, keep every hesitation. Voice: [pause], [laughs], [restarts], [um], [you know?]. Sound: room tone, background ambience, no score during talking. Prompt: "Handheld smartphone footage, eye level, slightly off-center framing, natural window light, 30fps, natural ungraded, subject speaking directly to camera, 9:16 vertical, authentic and relatable" Pitfalls: Any production value — one gimbal move or one dramatic light and the authenticity collapses. Perfect framing — real people don't compose shots. Removing room tone — the background noise is the authenticity signal. Removing hesitations — the pauses are the credibility.
CAMERA BODY
Smartphone — The UGC signal. This is a real person, not a production. Keyword: "smartphone footage" / "vertical mobile phone camera"
LENS
Standard (35–50mm) — Closest to human eye. Trust, naturalism, documentary, UGC. The invisible lens. Also the correct choice for vertical format. Keyword: "35mm standard lens, natural perspective"
FOCUS
Shallow focus — Only the subject is sharp. Background dissolves into bokeh. Only this matters. Keyword: "shallow focus, subject sharp, background bokeh"
SHOT SIZES
Medium close-up (MCU) — Chest up. Expression dominant. Standard dialogue frame. Keyword: "medium close-up" Medium shot (MS) — Subject from waist up. The workhorse frame. Keyword: "medium shot" Clean single — One subject, nothing competing for attention. Background fully blurred or empty. Complete audience focus on performance. Keyword: "clean single, isolated subject, background emptied or fully blurred"
CAMERA ANGLES
Eye level — Neutral. Democratic. The audience is an equal. Keyword: "eye level"
CAMERA MOVEMENTS
Handheld — Camera held by operator with natural organic movement. Authenticity signal. The instability says: this is real. Keyword: "handheld, cinéma vérité" Static (locked off) — No movement. Forces the audience to sit with something. Stillness commands attention because everything in modern media moves. Keyword: "locked off, static camera"
FRAME RATE
30fps — Broadcast standard. Slightly more immediate than 24fps. Television, live events.
LIGHTING
Overcast / diffused natural light — Soft directionless light. Honest. Documentary, UGC, confessional. Keyword: "overcast light, soft diffused, shadowless" High-key lighting — Brightly lit, minimal shadows. Fill light eliminates darkness. Comedy, commercial, UGC. The world has no secrets. Keyword: "high-key lighting, bright and shadowless"
LIGHTING THE FACE
Frontal — Open, honest, nothing hidden. Interviews, UGC, trust.
COLOR GRADES
Natural / ungraded — No grade. Raw. UGC authenticity, documentary truth. Keyword: "natural ungraded, no color correction"

ASPECT RATIO
9:16 (vertical) — Mobile-first format. Specific compositional rules apply — see below. Keyword: "vertical 9:16 framing, mobile format"
VERTICAL FORMAT COMPOSITION
9:16 is not horizontal cinema rotated. It is a different compositional language. Subject placement — Center frame or upper third. The lower third belongs to captions and platform UI. Placing subjects in the lower third will result in them being obscured by platform overlays. Text safety zones — Top 15% and bottom 20% of frame are UI territory on every major platform. All important visual content must live in the middle 65%. Action in the vertical plane — Horizontal movement exits frame almost immediately and feels purposeless in vertical format. Action, gesture, and expression should move vertically or toward and away from camera — a subject rising, falling, jumping, reaching upward, or moving toward the lens uses the frame correctly. Depth over width — Horizontal cinema uses width to separate subjects. Vertical format uses depth — one subject closer, one further, stacked in the frame. Two subjects side by side in vertical format are compositionally awkward. Camera movement — Tilt is the natural movement. Pan traverses the narrow dimension quickly and feels constrained. Push-in, pull-out, rise, and descent all work well. Horizontal tracking feels wrong unless used very briefly. Lenses — Use standard lenses (35–50mm equivalent). Anamorphic lenses produce horizontal flares and wide aspect ratio — both work against vertical format. Keyword: "vertical 9:16 framing, subject centered or in upper third, action in vertical plane"
TEXTURE AND GRAIN
Clean digital — No grain. Present-tense, clinical, precise. Keyword: "clean digital, grain-free"
SOUND DESIGN
Room tone / ambience — Every location has a frequency. A hospital sounds different from a forest. The absence of room tone makes any location feel like a studio. Documentary and interview — Room tone is sacred. Its absence makes every location feel like a studio.
